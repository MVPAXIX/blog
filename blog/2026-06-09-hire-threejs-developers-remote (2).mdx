## Why Three.js Developers Are Hard to Find

Standard JavaScript developers are abundant. Three.js developers are not.

The skill gap comes from the 3D mathematics component. Most web developers never have reason to work with vectors, quaternions, camera projections, or scene graphs. Three.js requires all of these. The people who have these skills typically come from one of three backgrounds:

- Game development (Unity, Unreal, Godot)
- Computer graphics academia (computational geometry, graphics programming)
- Self-taught 3D visualization (usually designers who went deep into code)

None of these paths are common. That is why the supply is thin—and why you need a smarter hiring process.

## What to Look For in a Three.js Developer

### Technical requirements

A genuine Three.js developer should be able to:

- Set up and manage scenes, cameras, and renderers without boilerplate confusion
- Work with `BufferGeometry` and understand why it matters for performance
- Implement custom `ShaderMaterial` with GLSL for visual effects
- Optimize frame rates using techniques like instanced meshes, LOD (level of detail), and frustum culling
- Handle asset pipelines: loading GLTF/GLB models, compressed textures, and draco geometry
- Integrate Three.js into a React application using `react-three-fiber` or direct DOM mounting
- Debug rendering performance with browser devtools and Spector.js

### Soft requirements

Three.js projects are almost always creative and highly iterative. The developer should:

- Be comfortable with ambiguous design briefs
- Communicate clearly about what is technically feasible
- Have a portfolio of shipped work—live URLs you can visit and inspect

## The Technical Interview: What to Test

A strong Three.js interview focuses on **live problem-solving**, not trivia. Good questions:

**Conceptual:**
- "Explain the difference between a perspective camera and an orthographic camera. When would you use each?"
- "How would you approach a scene with 10,000 moving objects without tanking the frame rate?"
- "What is a texture atlas and when is it worth the complexity?"

**Practical (give them a CodeSandbox):**
- Build a rotating cube with a custom fragment shader that pulses between two colors
- Load a GLTF model, apply an environment map, and implement a simple orbit control
- Find the performance bottleneck in a given scene and explain how to fix it

What you are looking for is not just the answer—it is the reasoning process. Great Three.js developers think out loud about tradeoffs.

## Why Remote Three.js Developers Work Well

Three.js projects tend to be:

- **Asynchronous-friendly**: Most of the work happens in solo coding sessions, not constant collaboration
- **Portfolio-visible**: You can always load a URL and see exactly what they built
- **Measurable**: Frame rate, load time, and visual fidelity are objective metrics

Remote Three.js developers who are genuinely skilled tend to have strong portfolios of personal projects—experiments on CodePen, published npm packages, open-source contributions to Three.js or `react-three-fiber`. These are easy to evaluate regardless of where in the world the developer lives.

## The Hiring Mistake Most Teams Make

The most common error is hiring a JavaScript generalist and expecting them to "pick up Three.js on the job."

Three.js can be learned—but the 3D math underneath it cannot be easily self-taught under production deadlines. A developer without the spatial reasoning foundation will struggle with performance problems, shader debugging, and complex scene management in ways that become expensive.

Hire for the 3D skill first. Everything else can be trained.
