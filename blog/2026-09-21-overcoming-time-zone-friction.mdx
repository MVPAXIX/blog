# Overcoming Time Zone Friction: Building a True Follow-the-Sun Product Roadmap

Employing people in distant time zones will almost always play out in one of two ways: Either there is a 24/7, clockwork product-development process, or you are stuck waiting 12 hours for one Slack response[cite: 16]. To achieve the “follow the sun” model, which requires flawless work transition between continents, you need to remove the friction from daily activities[cite: 16].

## Stop Relying on Real-Time Meetings

A 7hr difference will make your team unproductive if everyone needs to be in the same Zoom meeting to make a decision[cite: 16].

- **Use Async:** All task, feature specs and bug reports must be well documented[cite: 16]. Utilizing effective asynchronous work strategies, the task context should be very clear on the Nairobi ticket if a developer logs on as a developer logs off in San Francisco[cite: 16].
- **Establish and agree on Handoff Protocols:** Convene end-of-day updates[cite: 16]. You keep the next time zone moving without delay with a simple three-line summary (What got shipped / What is blocked / What is next.)[cite: 16].

## Take a Look at Overlap Hours, NOT Full Overlap

It is not necessary for team members to work on the night shift to remain connected[cite: 16]. Try to overlap the 2–3-hour window as much as possible for your distributed engineering teams[cite: 16].

- That time is only to be used for unblocking, code review and high priority alignment[cite: 16].
- Limit deep-focus time to the non-overlapping hours[cite: 16].

## Focus on Output and Not Hours Online

There's no better way to mess with a global team than by having to see how their mouse moves; or when they're expected to be constantly replying to status updates[cite: 16].

- This may require setting up clear sprint output metrics, but as long as developers feel they are in control and have known expectations, geographic boundaries go out the window, fostering a healthy remote work culture and boosting remote talent retention[cite: 16].
