A bad overseas hire rarely announces itself in the interview. It announces itself in week six, when work is late, updates have stopped, and you are doing the job you hired someone else to do.

The interview is the problem. Interviews measure how well someone performs in a conversation, which is a real skill and almost never the skill you are paying for. Every test below exists to measure something an interview cannot see.

## Test 1: The written work sample

**What it catches:** People who describe work convincingly but cannot produce it.

Give every serious candidate a small, paid, realistic task drawn from the actual role. A developer ships a small feature against your conventions. A VA takes a messy inbox scenario and produces the plan and the replies. A bookkeeper reconciles a deliberately imperfect statement.

Rules that make this work:

- **Keep it under three hours** and pay for it. Unpaid multi-day assignments filter for the desperate rather than the good.
- **Use a real problem**, ideally one you have already solved, so you can compare against a known answer.
- **Include one ambiguity on purpose.** The best signal is not whether they resolve it correctly — it is whether they notice it and say so.

That last point is the whole test. Candidates who deliver confidently on a flawed brief without flagging the flaw will do exactly the same thing on your real work.

## Test 2: The asynchronous communication test

**What it catches:** People who are excellent live and invisible in writing.

This one costs you nothing extra: judge the hiring process itself as a work sample. Across your email and message exchanges, watch for:

- Response time to a non-urgent message during their stated working hours
- Whether they answer the question you actually asked
- Whether they ask a clarifying question before starting, or guess
- Whether the writing is clear enough to act on without a follow-up call

Someone who needs a call to explain what they meant in a message will need one every week, at whatever hour suits your time zone. In a distributed team, writing clearly is not a soft skill. It is the interface.

## Test 3: The specificity probe

**What it catches:** Inflated CVs and borrowed credit.

Take one claim from the CV and go three questions deep on it.

> "You improved the reporting process. Walk me through what it looked like before."
>
> "What did you change first?"
>
> "What broke, and what did you do about it?"

People who did the work answer these easily and get more interesting as you go deeper — they remember the annoying details, the thing that failed, the workaround. People who were adjacent to the work get vaguer with each question and retreat to general principles.

Do this for exactly one claim, not all of them. It takes ten minutes and is the single most reliable interview technique we use.

## Test 4: The reference check that is actually a reference check

**What it catches:** Patterns the candidate will not mention and you cannot see.

Most reference checks are a formality because most questions invite a polite non-answer. Two questions change that:

> "What did they need from a manager to do their best work?"

This is phrased as helpful, so people answer honestly. The answer tells you whether they need structure, autonomy, frequent feedback, or space — and whether you can provide it.

> "Would you hire them again for the same role?"

Ask it plainly, then stay quiet. The pause before the answer carries as much information as the answer.

Insist on speaking to a direct former manager, not a colleague or a friend. A candidate who cannot produce a single manager reference from several years of employment has told you something.

## Test 5: The logistics and stability check

**What it catches:** Problems that have nothing to do with talent and everything to do with whether the work happens.

Before you make an offer, confirm the practical foundation in writing:

- **Internet:** connection type, typical speed, and what the backup is. "Mobile hotspot" is an acceptable answer; no answer is not.
- **Power:** in regions with an unreliable grid, ask directly what happens during an outage. Candidates who work seriously from home already have an answer — an inverter, a battery, a nearby co-working space.
- **Workspace:** somewhere they can take a client call without interruption.
- **Hours:** their working day in their own local time, and the overlap they are committing to.
- **Other commitments:** are they taking on another full-time role? Ask plainly. Everyone finds out eventually.
- **Payment and compliance:** how they will be paid, in what currency, and under what contract structure.

None of this is glamorous, and it is the reason a surprising share of overseas placements fail. Talent is abundant. Reliable working conditions have to be verified.

## Why these five

Between them, these tests cover the five ways an overseas hire actually goes wrong: they cannot do the work, they cannot communicate it, they did not do what the CV claimed, they have a pattern their references know about, or their setup does not support the job.

Run all five and you have removed most of the risk. Skip them and you are relying on a conversation to predict months of unsupervised work.

This is the process Zemenay runs before a candidate ever reaches your shortlist — work samples, written communication, deep reference checks, and a verified working setup — which is why our placements are presented as ready to start rather than ready to interview.
