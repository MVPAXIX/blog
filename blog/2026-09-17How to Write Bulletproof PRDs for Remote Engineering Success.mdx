  <introduction>
    90% of software bugs are found in code, and 90% of offshore development delays occur in the Product Requirement Document (PRD)[cite: 7]. With time zone-driven, dispersed engineering teams, "side by side" chat and "filling in the blanks" during live sessions is not an option[cite: 7]. Often, vague specifications become incorrect hypotheses, dead sprint cycles, and expensive rework[cite: 7]. To deliver fast product features without having to explain things in real time continuously, product managers need to create product specs that can be executed using effective asynchronous work strategies[cite: 7].
  </introduction>

  <main_content>
    <section>
      <sub_headline>Why Traditional PRDs Fail Distributed Teams</sub_headline>
      <body>In an office setting, a vague requirement in a generic PRD is resolved during a 2-minute conversation at someone's desk[cite: 7]. In a cross-border engineering configuration, it slows down progress for 12–24 hours until the developer receives a response from across time zones[cite: 7]. Traditional PRDs fail distributed engineering teams because they leave business logic and boundary rules unassigned, relying on general statements like "User should be able to filter search results easily"[cite: 7].</body>
      <list_item>High-Level Hand-Waving: Broad statements leave business logic and boundary rules completely unassigned[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Edge Cases: Failing to account for error states or offline behavior forces developers to assume what the application should do when edge cases arise[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Ambiguous Acceptance Criteria: Without clearly defined criteria for what is "done," engineers might decide a feature is complete after meeting only half of the expectations[cite: 7].</list_item>
    </section>

    <section>
      <sub_headline>Anatomy of an Asynchronous-First PRD</sub_headline>
      <body>A bulletproof PRD serves as a standalone blueprint[cite: 7]. It should be readable by any engineer in any time zone on Day 1 of the sprint and implemented without needing to verify basic context[cite: 7].</body>
      <list_item>Problem &amp; Context: The business "why," target user persona, and measurable goal provide developers with architectural intuition for making smart "micro" decisions[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>User Flow &amp; Wireframes: Embedded Figma designs featuring complete UX states (hover, disabled, loading) avoid visual guessing and design debt during implementation[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Explicit Acceptance Criteria: Functional requirements structured as a Given/When/Then checklist provide QA and developers with a common, binary measure of success[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Out of Scope: An explicit list of items excluded from the ticket/sprint prevents scope creep and locks sprint capacity[cite: 7].</list_item>
    </section>

    <section>
      <sub_headline>4 Rules for Writing Clear Specifications</sub_headline>
      <body>Adhering to strict spec-writing rules bridges communication gaps and protects engineering velocity[cite: 7]:</body>
      <list_item>Use BDD-Style Statements as Acceptance Criteria: Structure requirements clearly (e.g., Given a user is logged in with an expired subscription, When they click "Upgrade Plan", Then display the billing modal with the annual plan selected)[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Document the "Sad Paths": Clearly define behavior for API timeouts, invalid file imports, or empty database responses so developers don't have to guess error handling[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Record a Video Walkthrough: Include a 2-minute screen recording walking through the Figma prototype to provide visual context that text cannot capture, facilitating smoother cross-cultural team integration[cite: 7].</list_item>
      <list_item>Lock Scope: Finalize and lock the PRD scope prior to sprint kickoff; capture mid-sprint edge cases as follow-up backlog tickets to maintain effective remote team management[cite: 7].</list_item>
    </section>
