# English Proficiency vs. Technical Skill: Finding the Sweet Spot in Global Recruitment

**Technical ability gets a candidate noticed. Communication helps them succeed.**

For companies building remote teams across borders, hiring is no longer simply about finding the person with the strongest technical résumé. A developer may be exceptional at writing code, but if they struggle to explain decisions, understand requirements, collaborate asynchronously, or communicate blockers, their technical potential can become difficult to translate into business results.

At the same time, strong English alone does not make someone a great engineer.

The real opportunity is to find the **sweet spot between technical competence, communication, collaboration, and cultural adaptability**.

For companies looking beyond traditional hiring markets, this balance can unlock access to highly capable professionals while creating stronger, more connected remote teams.

---

## 1. Why Technical Brilliance Alone Can Fall Short in Remote Teams

In a traditional office, communication can happen naturally throughout the day. A developer can walk over to a colleague's desk, clarify a requirement, or quickly explain why a particular approach was chosen.

Remote teams do not have that luxury.

When people work across countries, time zones, and cultures, communication becomes part of the technical workflow itself.

Remote professionals often need to:

- Document their work clearly.
- Explain technical decisions in pull requests and project documentation.
- Communicate blockers before they become major problems.
- Ask precise questions when requirements are unclear.
- Participate effectively in virtual meetings.
- Provide clear progress updates to managers and clients.
- Collaborate with teammates they may never meet in person.

A highly skilled developer who cannot communicate effectively may still produce excellent code, but the surrounding team can lose time trying to understand requirements, decisions, and project status.

**In remote work, communication is not separate from productivity. It is part of productivity.**

---

## 2. The Strategic Value of Strong English Communication

English is widely used across international technology teams, making English proficiency particularly valuable when employees work with overseas clients, managers, and colleagues.

Strong communication can help professionals:

### Understand requirements accurately

Misunderstanding a requirement can lead to unnecessary development work, repeated revisions, and missed deadlines.

A professional who can confidently clarify ambiguous requirements is more likely to build the right solution the first time.

### Build trust with international clients

Clients want more than someone who can complete a technical task. They want professionals who can explain progress, communicate challenges, and contribute ideas.

Clear communication helps transform a remote professional from a task executor into a trusted member of the client's team.

### Collaborate asynchronously

Remote teams frequently depend on written communication through project management tools, email, documentation platforms, Git workflows, and team chats.

The ability to communicate clearly without relying on face-to-face interaction is therefore a major advantage.

### Solve problems proactively

Good communication makes it easier for professionals to say:

> "Here is the problem, here is what I have tried, and here is the approach I recommend."

That type of communication demonstrates ownership rather than simply reporting a problem.

---

## 3. But English Should Not Replace Technical Evaluation

There is an important distinction between **communication ability** and **technical ability**.

A candidate should not be considered highly qualified simply because they speak fluent English.

For technical roles, employers still need evidence of:

- Problem-solving ability.
- Relevant programming or engineering skills.
- Understanding of software development principles.
- Experience with appropriate tools and technologies.
- Ability to work with APIs, databases, cloud platforms, or other role-specific systems.
- Portfolio or project experience.
- Ability to learn and adapt to new technologies.

The goal should not be:

**English OR technical skill.**

It should be:

**Technical skill + communication + collaboration + reliability.**

That combination creates much stronger long-term hiring outcomes.

---

## 4. Finding the Sweet Spot During Recruitment

The best way to identify balanced candidates is to evaluate different capabilities at different stages of the hiring process.

### Step 1: Review technical evidence

Look beyond a résumé.

Review portfolios, GitHub activity where relevant, previous projects, certifications, technical assessments, and practical experience.

The objective is to determine whether the candidate can actually apply their knowledge.

### Step 2: Test communication naturally

A communication assessment does not need to feel like an English examination.

Instead, ask candidates to:

- Explain a project they have worked on.
- Describe how they would solve a technical problem.
- Explain a difficult concept to a non-technical client.
- Walk through a previous challenge and how they resolved it.
- Respond to a realistic workplace scenario.

This reveals much more than simply asking whether someone speaks English.

### Step 3: Evaluate collaboration

Technical professionals rarely work alone.

Consider how candidates:

- Receive feedback.
- Handle disagreement.
- Ask for clarification.
- Communicate delays.
- Explain mistakes.
- Work with designers, project managers, clients, and other developers.

### Step 4: Assess reliability and ownership

A strong remote employee should be able to manage responsibilities without constant supervision.

Look for evidence of:

- Accountability.
- Consistent communication.
- Time management.
- Meeting commitments.
- Proactive problem-solving.
- Willingness to learn.

## 5. Why This Matters for Global Hiring

Global recruitment gives companies access to talent beyond their immediate geographic market.

For employers, this can mean access to professionals with different experiences, perspectives, educational backgrounds, and technical strengths.

For professionals, it can create opportunities to work with international companies without relocating.

However, successful global hiring requires more than simply matching a résumé to a job description.

The candidate needs to be able to **integrate into the team's way of working**.

That means understanding expectations, communicating clearly, collaborating across cultures, and delivering quality work consistently.

This is where a structured talent-vetting process becomes valuable.

## 7. A Better Way to Think About "Talent"

Recruitment is sometimes treated as a search for the candidate with the most impressive résumé.

But the strongest candidate on paper is not always the strongest candidate for the team.

Imagine two developers:

**Candidate A**

- Exceptional technical skills.
- Strong portfolio.
- Struggles to explain decisions.
- Rarely communicates blockers.
- Finds client communication difficult.

**Candidate B**

- Very strong technical skills.
- Strong portfolio.
- Communicates clearly.
- Asks thoughtful questions.
- Explains technical concepts well.
- Comfortable collaborating with international teams.

If the role requires continuous remote collaboration, Candidate B may create more value for the organization even if Candidate A scored slightly higher on a technical assessment.

The lesson is simple:

**The best hire is not necessarily the person who scores highest in one category. It is the person whose combined strengths match the realities of the role.**

---

## 8. What Employers Should Look For

When hiring remote technology professionals, consider evaluating candidates across a broader scorecard:

| Area | What to Evaluate |
|---|---|
| Technical skills | Role-specific knowledge and practical ability |
| Communication | Clarity, listening, writing, and verbal communication |
| Problem solving | Ability to analyze and resolve challenges |
| Collaboration | Teamwork, feedback, and interpersonal skills |
| Adaptability | Ability to learn and work with changing requirements |
| Reliability | Ownership, time management, and consistency |
| Remote readiness | Ability to work independently and communicate asynchronously |

This creates a more complete picture of a candidate than a résumé or interview alone.

---

## 9. The Future of Global Recruitment Is About Balance

Technology has made it possible for companies to build teams across borders. But technology alone does not make distributed teams successful.

People do.

And people work best when they can combine expertise with communication, ownership, and collaboration.

For employers, this means expanding the definition of what makes someone "qualified."

For professionals, it means developing both sides of the equation:

**Build your technical skills. Build your communication skills. Then learn how to use both together.**

That is where global opportunities become much more accessible.

---

The debate between English proficiency and technical skill is ultimately the wrong question.

Strong global teams do not need to choose one over the other.

They need professionals who can **build, explain, collaborate, adapt, and deliver**.
